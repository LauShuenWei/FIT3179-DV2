Wild Australia

Where, When and Under What Conditions Is Wildlife Observed?

Citizen-science observations provide a valuable view of when and where wildlife is recorded across Australia. This visualisation explores how recording patterns differ across animal groups, locations, seasons and climate conditions.

01

Wildlife Across Australia

Wildlife records are not distributed evenly across the continent. Some locations contain far more recorded observations and species than others.

Wildlife Recording Activity Across Australia

Spatial patterns of citizen-science observation records and recorded species richness, 2018–2024

Each square represents a 0.25° grid cell. Darker cells indicate a higher value for the selected measure. Observation records show the number of wildlife records submitted within a cell, while species richness shows the number of distinct species recorded. These patterns reflect recording activity rather than wildlife population density. Hover over a cell for details.

Queensland Leads Recorded Species Richness

Distinct wildlife species recorded by Australian state or territory, 2018–2024

Queensland records 1,310 distinct species in this dataset, 318 more than Western Australia and 370 more than New South Wales. Recorded richness is influenced by geographic area and observation effort, so differences between states should not be interpreted as direct measures of total biodiversity. Hover over a state or bar for details.

02

Different Animals, Different Places

Birds, mammals, reptiles and amphibians show different recording patterns across Australian states and across the landscape.

Birds Dominate Australia’s Wildlife Records

Share of wildlife observation records by animal group, Australia, 2018–2024

Birds account for nearly three-quarters of all wildlife observation records in this dataset. Reptiles and mammals each contribute around one-tenth, while amphibians represent the smallest share. These proportions reflect recording activity rather than relative wildlife population abundance.

The Bigger Picture

Places and months with more wildlife observations also tend to reveal more recorded species. This highlights an important feature of citizen-science data: greater observation effort can reveal greater recorded diversity.

What does this tell us?

Australian wildlife records show strong geographic, seasonal and environmental patterns, but they also reflect where and when people are observing. These data should therefore be interpreted as patterns in recorded observations, rather than direct estimates of wildlife population size or abundance.

Data & Methodology

Wildlife observations are based on research-grade iNaturalist records accessed through GBIF for birds, mammals, reptiles and amphibians recorded in Australia from 2018 to 2024.

Climate information is derived from SILO Australian climate datasets, including monthly rainfall and temperature conditions. Wildlife observations were spatially matched to the nearest SILO climate grid location.

These visualisations describe patterns in recorded observations. Citizen-science records are influenced by where and when people observe wildlife, so they should not be interpreted as direct measures of wildlife population size or abundance.
